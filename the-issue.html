Understanding technology-facilitated violence

Phones, apps and the internet have opened new doors for women and girls. They have also given abusers new ways to reach, watch and control them.

Definition

What is TFGBV?

It is broader than "online abuse". Technology can be the weapon itself, or it can make violence that already exists more severe, more persistent and harder to escape.

In simple terms

Any act of gender-based violence that is committed, assisted, amplified or enabled through digital technologies, the internet or electronic devices.

Technology is used to

  • Harm
  • Threaten
  • Control
  • Sexually exploit
  • Intimidate
  • Abuse

Two sides of the problem

How technology is involved

Technology as the weapon

Some abuse only exists because of technology: a deepfake video, spyware hidden on a phone, or a hacked account used to impersonate someone.

Technology as the amplifier

Other abuse starts offline but grows online. Threats reach someone at any hour, humiliation spreads to thousands, and control follows them everywhere.

Forms it takes

Repeated unwanted contact, insults, threats or sexual comments sent through WhatsApp, TikTok, Instagram, comments or email. It often aims to shame, frighten or push women and girls out of online spaces.

Warning signs: messages from unknown or fake accounts, coordinated pile-ons, sexual comments on your posts.

Sharing, or threatening to share, private sexual images or videos without the consent of the person shown. It is sometimes called "revenge porn", a term that wrongly suggests the survivor did something to deserve it.

Warning signs: threats to "expose" you, or images appearing in groups or on sites you never shared them with.

Using artificial intelligence to create fake but realistic sexual images, videos or audio of a real person, then spreading them to humiliate, silence or blackmail her.

Warning signs: content showing you in situations that never happened, or odd blurring and distortion around faces.

Monitoring someone's movements, messages or online activity without their knowledge, for example through shared location, hidden tracking apps or repeatedly checking their accounts.

Warning signs: someone knowing where you are or what you said in private, or unfamiliar apps and devices.

Gaining access to someone's social media, email, mobile money or bank accounts to read private messages, impersonate them, lock them out or steal from them.

Warning signs: password reset messages you did not request, posts sent from your account, or logins you do not recognise.

Sustained abuse, intimidation or threats, especially threats of sexual violence or physical harm, delivered through digital channels.

Warning signs: threatening messages, abusive posts about you, or others being encouraged to harm you.

Using digital banking, mobile money or financial apps to control, monitor or exploit someone, such as taking over their accounts or demanding access to their earnings.

Warning signs: being pressured to share PINs, money going missing, or a partner controlling every mobile money transaction.

Publishing someone's private information, such as their home address, workplace, phone number or family details, to intimidate them or put them in danger.

Warning signs: your personal details posted publicly, or strangers contacting you or turning up where you go.

Grooming, sextortion or coercing women and girls into producing or sending sexual material, often by building false trust and then using threats or blackmail.

Warning signs: a new contact moving quickly to private chat, requests for images, or threats once an image has been shared.

Using phones, GPS trackers, smart devices, passwords or spyware to control a partner's behaviour, communication and freedom.

Warning signs: demands for your passwords, a partner checking your phone, or limits on who you can contact.

Why it matters

The harm goes far beyond the screen

Because it happens through everyday tools, technology-facilitated violence can feel inescapable. It is often under-reported and its effects are long lasting.

Mental and emotional harm

Fear, anxiety, shame and trauma that can last long after the abuse stops.

Silencing and isolation

Many women and girls withdraw from online spaces, public debate and leadership.

Lost opportunities

Abuse can disrupt education, damage reputations and cost people their livelihoods.

Physical danger

Online threats, tracking and doxxing can escalate into violence offline.
